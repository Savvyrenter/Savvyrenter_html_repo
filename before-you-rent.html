Before you rent

Look a place up before you view it · know what to look for when you are standing in it
Summary Start this when you first see an advert and carry it with you to the viewing. Put the address in and it points you to the free public records for the place — the energy certificate, the agent, licensing, flood risk, crime — then turns what you find into a list of things to look for when you walk round. Nothing here costs money to look up. Nothing you type is stored or sent anywhere unless you save it to a file yourself.
Full explanation

A viewing lasts ten minutes and the agent is showing you the best of it. Fresh paint, a candle burning, the windows open. The things that make a home cold, damp or unsafe are rarely the things you are shown, and once you have signed, paid the deposit and moved your furniture in, walking away is far harder.

A surprising amount of what warns you is on free public records: the age of the building, how it is heated, what its walls are made of, who manages it. None of it proves a problem on its own. What it does is tell you where to look.

So every finding here is a pointer, never a verdict. An old building with solid walls is not necessarily damp — but it is worth looking behind the wardrobe. A flat in a converted house may have been converted perfectly — but only the council’s records can say whether it was signed off, and the page tells you that rather than guessing.

It only ever sends you to records that are free. Some cost a few pounds, like who owns the building; that is worth paying for once you are in a tenancy and need it, not for every place you look at, because a few pounds a viewing soon becomes your deposit.

Save it to a file after your research and open it again on your phone at the viewing. The same file will carry what you found into the moving in audit if you take the place.

Nothing you type is stored or sent anywhere. The postcode is used to fetch the same small area file everyone in that postcode district gets. Crime figures and the other records are only fetched or opened when you press their button. Close the page and it is gone, unless you saved it.

The place you are looking at

Answering an online advert? Keep a copy of it. Adverts are changed or taken down once a place is let, and what the advert said — the rent, what is included, the furniture, the date it is available — is what the agent can be held to later. Put the link in, open the advert, and save the page as a PDF: on a computer press Ctrl+P (Cmd+P on a Mac) and choose Save as PDF; on a phone use Share → Print, or take full-page screenshots. Then attach it here and it is kept inside your saved file.

Look it up before you go — all free

Energy certificate (EPC)

Find the certificate by postcode ↗ — public and free. Besides the band, it says roughly when the building went up, what the walls are made of and how it is heated, which is a lot of what you need to know about damp before you get there. If there is no certificate at all, the landlord needs one before letting.

The agent, and who looks after the building

Press Companies House to open the register with the name already searched. Look at the status line: active is what you want; proposal to strike off, liquidation or dissolved means do not hand over money. Every letting agent must belong to a redress scheme — check it on The Property Ombudsman · Property Redress Scheme. If they hold money they also need client money protection.

Licensing

A shared house with 5 or more people from 2 or more households always needs a licence, and many councils licence ordinary lets as well. Check by postcode ↗ · Find the council ↗ — councils keep a public register of licensed properties.

The area

Long-term flood risk ↗ (England) · Council tax band ↗ (England and Wales) · Street View ↗ — use the dates in the corner to step back through the years: scaffolding, roof work, stains down the outside walls or a conversion going on are all there to see.

At the viewing

Anything picked out in yellow is there because of something you found above. Mark each one as you go round, and take a photo of anything that worries you — on your own phone, so the date is on it.

Meters, the stopcock and the lines in

Ask the agent to show you each one. If you pay the bills you have to be able to read your meters, and your supplier has to be able to reach them. A stopcock you cannot get to is no use when a pipe bursts. And note which lines already come in — it decides what broadband you can have without new work.

Questions to send in writing afterwards

Ask by email, not on the doorstep, so the answers are on record. Change it to sound like you.
