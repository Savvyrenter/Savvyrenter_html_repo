Finding a home and applying

Can a landlord refuse to rent to me because I claim benefits?

Not in England any more. Since 1 May 2026 it has been unlawful for a landlord or letting agent to refuse you, or to put you off applying, because you receive benefits or have children. That covers adverts saying "No DSS" or "no benefits" and agents who simply won't book a viewing once they hear you claim Universal Credit. Councils can fine a landlord or agent up to £7,000. A landlord can still check you can afford the rent, but they must look at you as an individual rather than ruling out everyone on benefits. Keep screenshots of the advert or messages and report it to your council's private housing or trading standards team. Shelter publishes a complaint letter you can send.

Is it legal for a room advert to say only one nationality or race can apply?

Usually not. Refusing someone because of their race, nationality or ethnic origin is discrimination under the Equality Act 2010, and that includes adverts that say only people of one nationality need apply. There is a narrow exception: where the landlord or a near relative lives in the home and shares living space such as the kitchen with you, and the home is small, the rules on choosing who to let to don't apply in the same way. A letting agent or a landlord who doesn't live there can't rely on that. You can report a discriminatory advert to the website it's on, and get advice from the Equality Advisory and Support Service.

Are rental bidding wars banned? The agent keeps hinting I should offer more.

Yes. Since 1 May 2026 a landlord or agent in England must state the rent in the advert, and must not ask for, encourage or accept offers above it. Hinting that "other people are offering more" is exactly what the ban is aimed at. You don't have to offer above the advertised rent, and the landlord can't take a higher offer from someone else. Save the advert and any messages, and report the agent to your council (which enforces the rule) and to the agent's redress scheme.

How much rent in advance can a landlord ask for?

In England, since 1 May 2026, a landlord can ask for no more than one month's rent before the tenancy starts. Asking for six or twelve months up front, which used to be common for people without a guarantor, is no longer allowed. That includes international students without a UK guarantor. On top of that they can take a holding deposit of up to one week's rent and a tenancy deposit of up to five weeks' rent (six weeks if the yearly rent is £50,000 or more). Most other fees are banned under the Tenant Fees Act 2019.

What can I do if I can't get a guarantor to rent a flat?

Guarantors are still allowed, and with rent in advance now capped at one month, many agents have started asking for one more often. Options worth trying: a guarantor company (some agents refuse them, but many accept them); asking a landlord who isn't using an agent, since they are often more flexible; offering references from a previous landlord and your employer or university; your council's rent deposit or bond scheme, if you are on a low income or at risk of homelessness; and, for students, your university's own guarantor scheme. If you are about to have nowhere to live, contact the council's housing options team at least 56 days before, because they have a duty to help you.

The landlord pulled out after I paid a holding deposit. Can the agent keep it because I never moved in?

No. A holding deposit can be at most one week's rent. If the landlord decides not to go ahead, it must be refunded in full within 7 days. It can only be kept if you gave false information, failed a Right to Rent check, pulled out yourself, or didn't take reasonable steps to agree the tenancy by the deadline (normally 15 days). "You didn't move in" is not one of those reasons. Ask for it back in writing, giving a 7-day deadline. If it isn't paid, complain to the agent's redress scheme or make a small claim. Shelter has a letter template for exactly this.

I paid money for a flat and it was a scam. What can I do?

Call your bank straight away on the number on the back of your card. Banks can sometimes stop or reverse a payment if you act quickly, and many refund victims of authorised push payment scams. Then report it to the police's national fraud reporting service and to the website where the advert appeared. To avoid it next time: never pay anything before you have viewed the property in person and met the landlord or agent; be suspicious of a landlord who is "abroad" and will post the keys; and check who owns the property through a free Companies House search or by asking for proof. Rent well below the going rate is a common warning sign.

Can an agent keep my holding deposit because my references failed, or because the move-in kept getting delayed?

Only in limited cases, and they must explain why in writing. If a landlord or agent decides to keep a holding deposit, they must tell you in writing within 7 days of deciding, giving the reason. If they don't, they lose the right to keep it, even if they would otherwise have had a good reason. A failed reference only justifies keeping it if you gave false or misleading information. Being turned down for a reason you had no control over doesn't. If the delay was on the landlord's or agent's side and the deadline for agreeing the tenancy (normally 15 days) passed because of them, the whole deposit must come back within 7 days.

Can a letting agent charge me for references, credit checks, viewings, renewals or the tenancy agreement?

No. Under the Tenant Fees Act 2019, landlords and agents in England can't charge tenants for viewings, referencing, credit checks, drawing up the tenancy agreement, inventories, check-in or check-out, renewing the tenancy, or writing you a reference for your next landlord. The only payments they can ask for are: rent; a holding deposit of up to one week's rent; a tenancy deposit of up to five weeks' rent; a fee of up to £50 for changing the tenancy at your request; the costs of ending a tenancy early at your request; bills such as utilities and council tax; and set charges for a lost key or late rent. If you have paid a banned fee, ask for it back in writing. Trading standards can fine the agent, and you can claim the money back through the county court.

The rent, council tax band or bills in the advert were wrong. What can I do?

Letting agents must not mislead you about a property, including the rent, what the rent includes, the council tax band, or the size or features of the home. That is covered by consumer protection law and by the agents' own redress schemes. You can check the real council tax band for free on GOV.UK. Since 1 May 2026, the advertised rent is also the most a landlord can accept. Save a copy of the advert, then complain to the agent. If that doesn't resolve it, complain to its redress scheme or your council's trading standards team.

Can an agent make me use their cleaning company or insurer, or charge for keys or changes to my tenancy?

No to the cleaning company, and only limited charges for the others. Under the Tenant Fees Act a landlord or agent can't require you to buy a service from someone they choose, such as a named professional cleaning firm at the end of the tenancy, an inventory or check-in company, a particular insurance policy, or a repair-reporting app with a subscription, as a condition of renting. You only have to leave the place as clean as it was at the start. A lost key can be charged at the actual, reasonable cost of replacing it (with receipts), not an inflated set fee. A change to the tenancy that you asked for, such as swapping a tenant or guarantor, can cost no more than £50, or more only if the agent can show higher reasonable costs. There is no charge for correcting the agent's own mistakes, or for things like seeing your deposit certificate or using a repair-reporting website. Extra charges for paying rent by debit or credit card are also banned.

Can a landlord rent out a home with an F or G energy rating?

Not usually. In England and Wales a private landlord can't let a home with an Energy Performance Certificate rating below E, unless they have registered a valid exemption on the government's PRS Exemptions Register. You can look up any home's certificate for free on GOV.UK before you rent. If the rating is F or G and there is no exemption, report it to the council's trading standards or private housing team. A poorly insulated home costs more to heat and is more likely to suffer from damp.

The flat was advertised as bills included, but now the landlord says I've gone over a usage limit. Can they charge me?

Only if the limit was made clear to you before you agreed to rent. A fair-use cap hidden from the advert and not spelled out in the tenancy agreement is likely to be a misleading practice, and an unfair term isn't binding on you. If there is a valid cap, ask to see the actual meter readings and bills behind the charge. Where a landlord resells gas or electricity, they can't charge you more than they paid for it. Complain to the agent, then its redress scheme, or get advice from Citizens Advice.

How do I know my rent and deposit are safe with a letting agent?

Every letting agent in England that holds tenants' or landlords' money must belong to a Client Money Protection scheme. If the agent goes bust or misuses the money, the scheme can pay you back. The agent must display its certificate in its office and on its website, and give you a copy free if you ask. It must also belong to a redress scheme (The Property Ombudsman or the Property Redress Scheme). If an agent can't show you either one, don't hand over money, and report it to your council's trading standards team.

What should I do about gas and electricity meters when I move in?

On the day you get the keys, take dated photos of every meter reading (gas, electricity and water if metered) and send them to the suppliers. That way you don't end up paying for the last tenant's use or their debts. If the home has a prepayment meter, the landlord or agent should hand over the key or card. Ask for it before move-in day, especially in winter. Debt left on a prepayment meter by a previous occupant isn't yours: tell the supplier you've just moved in and it must remove it. When you leave, do the same again, and close or move your accounts on your last day.

Deposits

Does my landlord have to protect my deposit, and what happens if they didn't?

Yes. In England a landlord must put your deposit in a government-approved scheme (DPS, mydeposits or TDS) within 30 days of receiving it. Within the same 30 days they must also give you the "prescribed information" saying which scheme and how to get the money back. You can check online with each scheme using your postcode and surname. If they missed the deadline, you can claim compensation of between one and three times the deposit through the county court, either during the tenancy or up to six years after. A landlord who hasn't protected the deposit also has their ability to evict you restricted.

How long does a landlord have to return my deposit?

Once you and the landlord agree how much you'll get back, it must be returned within 10 days. If you can't agree, the disputed part stays protected in the scheme until it's settled, and the rest should be paid to you. If you hear nothing, write to the landlord or agent asking for the deposit back. If that gets no reply, contact the scheme directly: it can start the return process for you 14 days after the tenancy ends.

My landlord wants to take money off my deposit. How do I challenge unfair deductions?

The deposit is your money, so it's up to the landlord to prove a deduction is justified, not up to you to prove it isn't. A landlord can claim for unpaid rent, damage beyond fair wear and tear, and items missing from the inventory. They can't charge you to make the place better than it was when you moved in, or for the normal ageing of carpets, paint and furniture. Ask for an itemised list with receipts or quotes. Then compare it with the check-in inventory and your own photos and videos from move-in day. If you can't agree, use the deposit scheme's free dispute service (ADR). An independent adjudicator decides on the written evidence alone.

My landlord's deposit claim went up after I refused to settle privately. What happens now?

This happens more often than it should. An adjudicator looks at whether each deduction is backed by evidence. The landlord needs to show what the property was like at check-in, what it was like at check-out, and what it cost to fix. A claim that suddenly grows once you refuse a private offer will be judged on that evidence, not on the size of the number. Answer each item separately and point out where the landlord has no check-in evidence. Point out wear and tear, and anything that makes the place better than it was when you moved in. Keep your reply factual. The landlord can't take more than the deposit through the scheme. Anything above that, they would have to claim from you in court.

My landlord refused the deposit scheme's dispute service and says they'll take me to court. Can they?

Yes. The dispute service (ADR) only runs if both of you agree to it, so a landlord can refuse and go to the small claims court instead. If the deposit is in the custodial DPS scheme, the money stays with the scheme until you agree or a court decides. With an insured scheme, the landlord or agent holds the money but must pay the disputed amount to the scheme. Either way the deposit is safe. In a small claim the landlord still has to prove each deduction, and the court fee for a claim of this size is modest. If the landlord does nothing, you can apply to the scheme to release the money after a set period, so check your scheme's rules.

What is the best way to make sure I get my deposit back when I move out?

Most of it comes down to move-in day. Check the inventory line by line and write down everything it misses. Take dated photos and video of every room, including inside cupboards, the oven, windows, mattresses and carpets. Send it all to the landlord or agent by email within a few days, so there is a dated record that they received it. Report any repairs in writing during the tenancy. When you leave, clean properly, take the same photos again and return all the keys. A deposit dispute is decided on written evidence, and evidence gathered at the start is what usually wins it.

Can a landlord ask for a deposit bigger than five weeks' rent?

Not if the yearly rent is under £50,000, which covers most tenancies. The cap is then five weeks' rent, and six weeks' if the yearly rent is £50,000 or more. To work it out, take the monthly rent, multiply by 12, divide by 52, and multiply by 5. Anything above that is a banned payment and must be given back. Trading standards can fine the landlord or agent, and you can claim the excess back through the county court. Every penny of the deposit still has to be protected in an approved scheme within 30 days.

The landlord is taking money off my deposit, but there was never a check-in inventory. Can they do that?

They can try, but it's hard for them to win. To take money for damage, the landlord has to show the property was in a better condition when you moved in than when you left. A check-out report alone shows the end condition, not what changed. Without a check-in inventory, or dated photos from the start, adjudicators in the deposit schemes usually find that a deduction for condition hasn't been proved. Point this out in your reply, and use the scheme's free dispute service if the landlord won't back down.

What counts as fair wear and tear on a rented home?

Fair wear and tear is the normal decline that comes from living somewhere for a while, and a landlord can't charge your deposit for it. It includes carpets wearing thin in walkways, minor scuffs, faded paint and curtains, small marks around light switches, and loose fittings from normal use. The longer you lived there, the more wear is expected. Damage is different: burns, stains, holes in walls, broken items or pet damage. Even then, the landlord can only charge a fair share of the cost, allowing for the item's age. They can't charge you a new-for-old price, or charge you to have the place professionally cleaned to a higher standard than it was when you moved in.

Can my landlord charge an extra pet deposit on top of the normal deposit?

Only if the total stays within the legal cap. In England every deposit, including any "pet deposit", counts towards the maximum of five weeks' rent (six weeks if the yearly rent is £50,000 or more). If the normal deposit already reaches the cap, an extra pet deposit isn't allowed. A landlord can agree a slightly higher rent because of a pet, but that has to be the rent itself, not a separate fee. Since 1 May 2026 you have the right to ask for a pet, and a landlord must not unreasonably refuse.

Rent and rent increases

How often can my landlord put the rent up, and how much notice do they have to give?

In England, since 1 May 2026, your landlord can only raise the rent once a year. They must use the official form (Form 4A, the section 13 notice) and give you at least two months' notice. Any rent review clause in your agreement can no longer be used to put the rent up another way. The new rent must not be more than the open market rent. If you think it is, you can challenge it at the First-tier Tribunal, but you must apply before the date the increase starts. The tribunal can't set the rent higher than the landlord proposed.

How do I challenge a rent increase at tribunal, and is it worth it?

Apply to the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) before the date on your Form 4A when the new rent starts. There is no fee to apply. The tribunal works out the open market rent for your home as it is now. It ignores improvements you made yourself, and it takes account of disrepair the landlord has failed to fix. It's worth applying if similar homes nearby are advertised for less than the new figure. Save adverts for comparable homes as evidence. Under the new rules the tribunal can lower the increase or confirm it, but it can't raise it above what the landlord asked for. Keep paying your current rent while you wait.

My flat is being advertised for less than the rent I've been asked to pay to stay. Can they do that?

Since 1 May 2026 tenancies in England no longer end and "renew" on new paperwork. They simply roll on. The only way a landlord can raise your rent is the yearly Form 4A notice, and you don't have to sign a new agreement at a higher rent. If your own flat, or one just like it in the same building, is being advertised for less than the landlord wants from you, save the advert. It is strong evidence for a tribunal that the increase is above the market rent.

Can the letting agent put my rent up by sending an email?

No. An email or a letter saying "your rent is going up" is not enough. The increase has to be made on Form 4A, with at least two months' notice, no more than once a year. The only alternative is for you to agree to the new rent in writing. Be careful: paying the higher amount without objecting can be treated as agreeing to it. If you haven't had a valid Form 4A, reply in writing saying you don't agree and will keep paying the current rent.

My landlord has died and nobody has contacted me. Do I still have to pay rent?

Yes. The tenancy carries on and the rent is now owed to the landlord's estate. If you stop paying, the executors can claim the arrears later, and it could put your home at risk. If you don't know who to pay, set the rent aside each month in a separate account, so you can show it was never spent. Write to the letting agent if there is one. You can also look up who owns the property on the Land Registry (a small fee), or search the probate records once probate has been granted. Repairs are still the estate's responsibility, and the council can act if they are not done.

How much can a landlord charge me for paying rent late?

Very little, and only after 14 days. Under the Tenant Fees Act a late payment charge is only allowed if your tenancy agreement includes one and the rent is at least 14 days overdue. Even then it's capped at interest of 3% above the Bank of England base rate on the overdue amount, worked out from the date the rent was due. A flat "£50 late fee" or similar is a banned payment, and you can ask for it back.

I've been sent an arrears warning but I've paid my rent. What should I do?

Don't ignore it, even if you know it's wrong. Ask the landlord or agent in writing for a full rent statement, and send them bank statements showing each payment and the date it cleared. Mistakes are common when payments are split, made by Universal Credit, or arrive a day after the due date. Ask them to correct their records and confirm in writing that you aren't in arrears. This matters because arrears can be used as a reason to evict, so the record needs putting straight early. If you have been charged twice for something, such as a repair the landlord already paid for, ask for it to be refunded the same way.

My landlord wants rent in cash. Do I get receipts or a rent statement?

Paying in cash isn't illegal, but always get a written, dated receipt every time. Without one you have no proof if the landlord later claims you are in arrears. If your rent is paid weekly, the landlord must by law give you a rent book. You can ask for a full rent statement at any time, for example for a Universal Credit claim or a mortgage or loan application, and a landlord or agent who refuses is giving you good grounds for a complaint. Paying by bank transfer is safer, because the bank keeps the record for you.

I paid rent for a period after my tenancy ended. Do I get it back?

Yes. Rent covers your time in the home. If you paid in advance for days after the tenancy legally ended and you handed the keys back, that money should be refunded. Ask in writing, setting out the end date and the amount worked out per day, and give a deadline. This is separate from your deposit, so it shouldn't be held back while the deposit is being sorted out. If it isn't paid, complain to the agent's redress scheme or make a small claim.

Repairs, damp and mould

How long does a landlord have to do repairs?

For private renters in England, the law says repairs must be done within a "reasonable time" after the landlord is told about them. What counts as reasonable depends on how serious the problem is. No heating or hot water in winter, a gas leak or a dangerous electrical fault should be dealt with within a day or so. A dripping tap can wait longer. Fixed deadlines for damp and mould (Awaab's Law) already apply to council and housing association homes, and will be extended to private renting, but no start date had been set as of October 2026. Always report repairs in writing and keep a copy. If nothing happens, ask the council's environmental health team to inspect.

My landlord says the mould is my fault and I should just bleach and paint it. Is that right?

Not necessarily. Mould is the landlord's job to fix when it comes from disrepair: leaks, rising damp, broken extractor fans, windows that won't open, or poor insulation. A home must also be fit to live in under the Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018, and serious damp makes it unfit. Painting over mould doesn't fix the cause. Report it in writing with photos, and say if anyone in the home is a baby, elderly, or has a health condition, because that raises the urgency. If the landlord won't act, ask the council's environmental health team to inspect. They can order the landlord to do the work, and being away abroad is no excuse.

There's a damp smell in my rented flat that comes and goes. What should I do?

A damp smell that comes and goes often points to a hidden leak, condensation behind furniture, or damp under floors or behind kitchen units. Note when it happens (after rain, after showers, in cold weather), take photos of any marks, and report it to the landlord in writing. Ask them to investigate the cause rather than just cleaning the surface. Keep a record of when you reported it. If the landlord does nothing and damp marks or mould appear, the council's environmental health team can inspect.

My rented flat has become unliveable. What are my options?

First, tell the landlord in writing what is wrong and that you consider the home unfit to live in. If there is an immediate danger, such as a gas leak, no heating in freezing weather, or water coming through the ceiling, ring the council's environmental health team the same day. Many councils have an out-of-hours number. The council can serve notices forcing repairs, and in serious cases ban the use of the property. If you have to leave, ask the council's housing team for help, because you may count as homeless while the home is unsafe. You can also claim compensation and repair orders through the courts under the Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act 2018. Keep paying rent unless you get advice first.

Can I stop paying rent until my landlord fixes things?

It's risky and usually not advised. Withholding rent puts you into arrears, and arrears are a reason the landlord can use to evict you. Rent arrears of three months is a ground for eviction the court must grant. Safer routes are to report the problem in writing, involve the council's environmental health team, and claim compensation or a rent reduction for the disrepair. There is also a legal process for paying for a repair yourself and deducting the cost from the rent, but it has strict steps. Get advice before you try it.

Can my landlord stop me drying clothes indoors or using a tumble dryer?

A tenancy agreement can include reasonable rules, but a term that leaves you with no realistic way to dry your washing can be unfair under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. That's especially so if there is no garden, no washing line and no space for a dryer. Unfair terms are not binding on you. If the landlord's worry is condensation and mould, the answer is proper ventilation, such as working extractor fans and windows that open, and that's the landlord's job. Ask in writing what alternative they are providing, and keep the reply.

What safety certificates should my landlord give me before I move in?

In England, before you move in, the landlord must give you a copy of the current gas safety record (if there is any gas in the home) and a copy of the latest electrical safety report (EICR). Both can be given electronically. The gas check must be repeated every year and the electrics every five years, and you should get a copy of each new report. Smoke alarms must be on every floor, and carbon monoxide alarms fitted in rooms with a fixed fuel-burning appliance (apart from gas cookers). You should also get the Energy Performance Certificate. If you haven't had these, ask in writing. Missing gas or electrical checks can be reported to the council.

My boiler has been broken for weeks. Should my landlord give me heaters?

Heating and hot water are repairs the landlord must keep working, and in cold weather a broken boiler should be treated as urgent. There isn't a specific rule that says "provide heaters", but asking for them is reasonable while you wait for a delayed repair, and most landlords will. If you have to run electric heaters or go elsewhere to wash, keep a record of the extra costs, because you can ask for them back along with a rent reduction for the time you went without. If the repair isn't done promptly, report it to the council's environmental health team. A home without adequate heating can be a serious hazard.

There were mice or bedbugs when I moved in. Who pays for pest control?

Can my landlord come into my home without asking?

Notices, leaving and eviction

I was given a section 21 notice. Is it still valid?

Almost certainly not. Section 21 "no-fault" notices were abolished in England on 1 May 2026. A section 21 served before that date could only be used if the landlord started court proceedings by 31 July 2026, or within six months of serving it if that was sooner, and that window has now closed. If you are only now being told to leave under an old section 21, it can no longer be used to evict you. A landlord now needs a legal reason on a section 8 notice, and still needs a court order.

I've been given a section 8 notice. Do I have to leave by the date on it?

No. A section 8 notice is the landlord saying they intend to apply to court. It isn't an order to leave. Only a court can order you out, and only a bailiff can remove you. The notice must use the official form, give legal grounds, and allow the right amount of notice for each ground. That is at least four months for selling or moving in, which can't be used in the first 12 months of the tenancy, and four weeks for serious rent arrears. Some grounds are mandatory, meaning the court must agree if they are proved. Others are discretionary, meaning the court decides whether it's reasonable. Check the notice carefully and get advice quickly, because there may be a defence.

What is Awaab's Law and does it apply to my home?

Awaab's Law sets legal deadlines for social landlords (councils and housing associations) in England to deal with dangerous hazards. Since 27 October 2025 they must deal with emergency hazards within 24 hours. For significant damp and mould they must investigate within 10 working days, send you a written summary of what they found, and start work within set timescales. It is being extended to more types of hazard in stages. It does not yet cover private renters: the law allows it to be extended to private renting, but no start date had been set as of October 2026. Report any hazard in writing and keep a copy, because the deadlines run from when the landlord is told.

Is it worth complaining to the Housing Ombudsman about repairs or damp and mould?

Often, yes. In its 2025-26 review, the Housing Ombudsman found that repairs made up 80% of the cases it looked at. Damp, condensation and mould made up about a third of repairs complaints, and it upheld more than three quarters of those. Leaks were upheld at a similar rate. Average compensation in upheld cases was £703, and landlords complied with 99% of orders on time. Keep photos, dates and copies of every report you made, because evidence of the landlord being told and not acting is what wins these complaints.

How can I check my housing association's complaints record?

The Housing Ombudsman publishes a performance report for every landlord with five or more decided cases, showing how often it was found at fault and in which areas. It also publishes reports of severe maladministration, special investigations into individual landlords, and "wider orders" requiring a landlord to fix problems affecting many residents. Look your landlord up on the Ombudsman's landlord performance page. A poor record is useful context to quote in your own complaint.
