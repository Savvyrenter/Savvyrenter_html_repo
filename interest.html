Full explanation

Most calculators put interest on one lump sum from one date. That is wrong for rent overpayments, because you paid month by month: the principal sum owed to you was increasing the whole time. Your first overpayment has been held the longest and earns the most interest; your last one the least.

This calculator adds each payment to the running balance on the day it was made, charges simple interest on that balance for the days until the next payment (balance × rate × days ÷ 365), and adds it all up. Interest is never charged on interest. A late first payment does not shift the rest — later payments are counted on the day the rent falls due.

Advanced mode lets you enter the date each payment actually cleared, rather than the date it was issued, so interest runs from the day the landlord had the money. Marking how each payment was made adds to the record. The more of each payment you record, the less there is to argue with.

The statement is laid out for the court: one line per payment, and, where the overpayment is still going on, a twelve-month run-on and a day-by-day table giving the exact figure due on any date, so it can be read off on the day of the hearing.

The 8% rate is the usual county-court rate (County Courts Act 1984, s.69); you can change it if the court directs a different one.

How the interest is worked out. Simple interest at 8% a year under section 69 of the County Courts Act 1984, claimed from the date each sum was paid. It is worked on an increasing principal sum: when each payment is made it joins the running balance, and interest for the days until the next payment is charged on that running balance only (balance × rate × days ÷ 365). Interest is never charged on interest. The same total results from charging every payment its own interest from its own date and adding them up. Whether to award interest, and at what rate, is for the court.
